Steadying yourself through the changes that reshape who you are, with culturally informed, evidence-based support.

In short: Therapy for life transitions gives you a supportive, structured space to process a major change, understand why it feels destabilising, and rebuild a sense of direction. Every life has hinge moments, and therapy for life transitions exists for exactly those seasons. A new job, a cross-country move, a wedding, a first baby, a divorce, an immigration to a new country, the loss of a parent, or the quiet of a home once the children have gone: each of these can leave you feeling unmoored even when you chose the change yourself. Many people who reach out for therapy for life transitions in California are not "in crisis" in any obvious way. They are simply standing at a threshold, sensing that the old version of their life no longer fits and the new one has not yet formed.

That in-between feeling is normal, and it is workable. Therapy for life transitions helps you name what is shifting, grieve what you are leaving behind, and make room for what is next, at a pace that feels humane. Because so many of our clients are high-achieving South Asian, BIPOC, and first-generation professionals, our approach to therapy for life transitions also holds the cultural and family expectations that quietly shape how a change lands. The best therapy for life transitions happens when you do not have to explain your whole background before you can begin. That is the space this practice is built to offer.

The transitions that bring people to therapy

Life transitions come in many shapes, and no single one is "too small" to matter. People often begin therapy around:

  • Career change: a new role, a layoff, a promotion into leadership, or a decision to leave a field entirely.
  • Moving: relocating cities, states, or countries and rebuilding a sense of home and belonging.
  • Marriage and partnership: merging two lives, families, and sets of expectations.
  • Parenthood: the identity shift of becoming a parent, including the postpartum season.
  • Divorce or a breakup: the end of a relationship you once organised your future around.
  • Immigration and bicultural living: adjusting to a new country while staying connected to the one you came from.
  • Loss and grief: the death of a loved one, or a loss that reshapes daily life.
  • The empty nest: rediscovering yourself and your relationships when children leave home.

Why change destabilises us, even when it is "good"

One of the most confusing parts of a transition is feeling low or anxious after something you wanted. A dream job, a happy marriage, a healthy baby: on paper these are wins, so the wobble that follows can feel like ingratitude. It is not. Any transition, welcome or unwelcome, asks you to let go of a familiar identity and the routines, roles, and relationships attached to it. Your nervous system reads that loss of the familiar as uncertainty, and uncertainty can look like anxiety, low mood, irritability, or trouble sleeping.

Transitions also tend to surface older material. A move can reawaken a childhood sense of not belonging. New parenthood can stir up how you yourself were parented. A career leap can amplify imposter feelings or people-pleasing patterns you thought you had outgrown. This is why therapy can be so useful during change: the present transition becomes a doorway to understanding patterns that have been with you for a long time.

How therapy helps you move through it

Therapy for life transitions is not about rushing you to the other side. It is about helping you stand steadily in the middle. In sessions, many people find it helps to:

  • Put words to a change that has felt vague or overwhelming, so it becomes something you can actually work with.
  • Separate the practical decisions from the emotional weight, so you are not carrying both at once.
  • Grieve what is ending without guilt, and honour what mattered about it.
  • Notice the beliefs a transition activates ("I should be handling this better", "I have to keep everyone happy") and gently question them.
  • Reconnect with your own values, so the next chapter is shaped by what you want, not only by others' expectations.

The modalities we may draw on

There is no single "correct" method for a transition. We match the approach to you, and often blend a few. Depending on what you are moving through, your therapist may draw on:

Internal Family Systems (IFS)

Transitions often leave you feeling pulled in different directions: one part of you is excited, another is terrified, another is quietly grieving. IFS offers a compassionate way to get to know these parts rather than fight them, so you can lead your own change with more steadiness.

EMDR for trauma held inside a transition

Sometimes a current change reactivates an earlier wound, or the transition itself is traumatic, such as a sudden loss or an unwanted ending. EMDR therapy can help the nervous system process those experiences so they stop hijacking the present. Our founder and several team members are trained in EMDR.

Psychodynamic and depth approaches

Because transitions tend to surface long-standing patterns, a psychodynamic lens helps connect what you are feeling now to your history, relationships, and the roles you have carried. That understanding often brings a lasting sense of relief, not just a coping strategy. Most of this work happens within individual therapy, tailored to your pace.

A cultural lens for immigrant and bicultural transitions

For first-generation, immigrant, and bicultural clients, transitions carry an extra layer. Choosing a partner, changing careers, setting a boundary with family, or building a life different from the one your parents imagined can feel like a private negotiation between cultures. There may be love and obligation and guilt all at once, and few spaces where that complexity is truly understood.

Frequently asked questions

Can therapy help even if my life change is a positive one?

Yes. Welcome changes such as a promotion, a wedding, or a new baby still ask you to let go of a familiar identity, and that can bring anxiety or low mood even alongside gratitude. Therapy for life transitions gives you space to hold both the joy and the wobble without judging yourself for feeling unsettled.

How long does therapy for a life transition usually take?

It varies from person to person. Some people find a shorter, focused course of sessions helps them steady through a specific change, while others use the transition as a starting point for deeper work on long-standing patterns. Your therapist will talk openly with you about pacing, and there is no fixed timeline we push you toward.

I am a first-generation or immigrant professional. Will my therapist understand the cultural side?

That is a core part of how we practise. Our founder is a South Asian, first-generation immigrant, and our team is intentionally diverse and culturally informed. We hold the family expectations and bicultural pressures that often accompany a transition, so you do not have to translate your background before we can help.

Do you offer online therapy for life transitions across California?

Yes. We see clients in person in West Los Angeles and Pasadena, and we also offer secure telehealth to anyone located anywhere in California. Many people find video sessions especially helpful during a transition such as a move or a demanding new role, when time and location are already stretched.
