How to Know If You Need Therapy

You do not have to be in crisis to benefit. A gentle, non-diagnosing guide to the signs it may be time.

Short answer: If you are asking how to know if you need therapy, that question is itself worth paying attention to. Many people wonder how to know if you need therapy long before anything feels like an emergency, and knowing how to know if you need therapy is less about a single symptom and more about a pattern that keeps repeating. You do not need to be in crisis to benefit. If low mood, anxiety, feeling stuck, or strain in your relationships has lasted for weeks, therapy can help you understand it and feel more like yourself.

One of the most common misconceptions about how to know if you need therapy is that things have to fall apart first. They do not. Across California, plenty of the people who start therapy are functioning well on the outside: holding down demanding jobs, showing up for family, meeting deadlines. And still, quietly, something feels heavy. Learning how to know if you need therapy often begins with noticing that gap between how put-together you look and how you actually feel inside. If you have been carrying that gap for a while, this guide is for you.

At Salonia Singh Therapy and Associates, a group practice with offices in West Los Angeles and Pasadena and secure telehealth throughout California, we meet people at exactly this stage, when they are still deciding. There is no test that settles the question of how to know if you need therapy for everyone. Instead, it helps to look honestly at a handful of signs. None of them is a diagnosis. Any one of them can simply mean you are human. But when several show up together, or when one has stayed for a long time, they may be worth talking through with a therapist.

Signs it may be time to consider therapy

Read these gently. This is not a checklist to pass or fail, and it is not a way to label yourself. It is a set of experiences many people find easier to face with support.

  • Persistent anxiety or low mood. Worry that will not switch off, a tight chest before ordinary tasks, or a flatness that has lingered for weeks rather than days. When it stops lifting on its own, it may be time to talk to someone.
  • Changes in sleep or appetite. Lying awake with a racing mind, waking exhausted, eating far more or far less than usual. The body often signals distress before we have words for it.
  • Feeling stuck. A sense of going through the motions, replaying the same problem, or knowing what you "should" do and being unable to move. Therapy can help you understand what is holding you in place.
  • Strain in your relationships. The same arguments on a loop, growing distance, resentment, or feeling unseen by the people closest to you. Individual work can shift these patterns even when you come on your own.
  • Using work or busyness to cope. Filling every hour so you never have to sit with how you feel. Productivity can be a very effective way to avoid pain, until it stops working.
  • People-pleasing and losing yourself. Saying yes when you mean no, managing everyone else's comfort, and slowly forgetting what you actually want. Many high-achievers know this one well.
  • Burnout. Cynicism, depletion, and the feeling that nothing you do is enough, even after rest. When the tank stays empty, therapy can help you find why.

Notice that none of these requires a rock-bottom moment. That is the point. You are allowed to seek support because life feels harder than it should, not only because it has become unbearable. If two or three of these have followed you around lately, that is a reasonable answer to how to know if you need therapy.

A quieter test. Ask yourself: is this getting in the way of my work, my relationships, or my sense of who I am, and has it lasted longer than a couple of weeks? If yes, you do not have to keep managing it alone. Talking to a professional is a reasonable, ordinary step, not a last resort.

You do not need to be in crisis to benefit

Therapy is not only for emergencies. Much of the work we do at Salonia Singh Therapy is with people who are, by most measures, doing fine, and who want to feel steadier, understand old patterns, or make a hard decision with more clarity. Individual therapy gives you a private, unhurried hour to think out loud with someone trained to help you make sense of it. Many people find that starting earlier, before things escalate, makes the work gentler and shorter.

It is also normal to feel unsure whether your reasons "count." They do. Career transitions, identity questions, imposter syndrome, self-esteem, family conflict, a breakup, or simply a season of feeling lost are all reasons people begin. If you are weighing the practical side of starting, our frequently asked questions cover fees, consultations, and how sessions work, and you can always reach out for a free consultation to talk it through with no pressure to commit.

A note on stigma in South Asian and immigrant communities

For many South Asian, BIPOC, and immigrant families, asking for help outside the family can feel loaded. There may be a quiet message that struggles stay private, that gratitude should cancel out difficulty, or that seeing a therapist means something has gone wrong with you. First-generation professionals often carry an extra weight: honoring the sacrifices that got them here while privately feeling anxious, unseen, or exhausted.

None of that means you are failing. It means you are navigating more than one world at once, and that is genuinely hard. The best therapy happens when you do not have to translate your world for your therapist. Our founder and several of our clinicians share this lived experience and understand the specific pull between duty, ambition, and self, which is why our approach is both culturally informed and grounded in evidence. If this is central to you, our culturally attuned therapy for South Asian clients and our practice's story may help you feel more at ease taking the first step.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if I need therapy or if I am overreacting?

A useful guide is duration and impact. If a difficult feeling has lasted longer than a couple of weeks, or if it is getting in the way of your work, relationships, or sense of self, that is a reasonable sign therapy may help. You are not overreacting for wanting support, and you do not need a diagnosis to begin. A free consultation can help you decide.

Do I have to be in crisis to start therapy?

No. Many people find therapy most helpful when they start before things reach a breaking point. Working on anxiety, feeling stuck, relationship strain, or burnout early often makes the work gentler. Therapy is for growth and understanding, not only for emergencies. That said, if you are in crisis, please contact 988 or 911 right away.

What kinds of issues do you help adults with?

Our clinicians support adults navigating anxiety, depression, childhood trauma, imposter syndrome, career transitions, identity questions, people-pleasing, self-esteem, family conflict, breakups, relationship issues, and major life changes. Many of our clients are high-achieving South Asian and BIPOC professionals, and our approach is both culturally informed and evidence-based.

Can I do therapy if I live outside Los Angeles or Pasadena?

Yes. In addition to our offices in West Los Angeles and Pasadena, we offer secure telehealth to clients anywhere in California. Research on telehealth in the field of mental health has found it can be effective for many people, and it makes consistent support far easier to fit around a demanding schedule.
