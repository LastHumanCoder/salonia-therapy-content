The short answer

Talking to South Asian parents about mental health tends to go better when you choose a calm moment, start with feelings they will recognize, such as stress, tiredness or worry, use words that fit your family, and share only what you are comfortable sharing. Expect questions or worry rather than instant understanding, and treat it as the start of a conversation, not a single talk. A culturally informed therapist can help you plan what to say.

For many children of South Asian immigrants, the idea of telling their parents about anxiety, depression or therapy feels harder than the struggle itself. You might imagine the questions: "What do you have to be sad about?" "Who have you told?" "Is this because of us?" You might worry they will blame themselves, or that a relative will find out by dinnertime.

Those fears make sense. They also do not have to decide for you. This guide looks at why the stigma runs deep in many desi families, how to plan and start the conversation, what to do if it goes badly, and how South Asian therapists and other culturally informed clinicians can help you prepare.

Why mental health can be hard to talk about at home

Every family is different, and South Asian communities are enormously diverse. Still, some themes come up often in our work:

  • Different vocabulary. Many parents grew up without words like anxiety or depression. Distress may be described as tension, weakness, stress or physical illness.
  • Mental health stigma. Struggling may be seen as a personal failing, a family embarrassment, or something that could affect marriage prospects.
  • Survival mindset. Parents who migrated and rebuilt their lives may have coped by pushing through, and may expect the same of you.
  • Privacy and reputation. Worry about what relatives or the community will think can make parents want to keep things quiet.
  • Faith and family as the answer. Some parents may suggest prayer, family support or a change in routine instead of therapy.

None of this means your parents do not care. Often it means they care in the only ways they were taught. Understanding that can help you approach the conversation with less fear and more patience.

Before the conversation

Decide what you want from it

Do you want them to understand why you have been withdrawn? To support you going to therapy? To stop certain comments? Or simply to know? Being clear about your goal helps you decide how much to share. You do not have to tell them everything, and you do not need their permission to look after your health as an adult.

Choose the person and the moment

Many people start with the parent, sibling or relative who feels safest. A calm, private moment works better than a family gathering or the middle of a disagreement. Some people find it easier to talk side by side, on a walk or in the car.

Pick words that will land

If "depression" might shut the conversation down, you could start with "I have been feeling very low and tired for a long time" or "I have been under so much stress that it is affecting my sleep." If your parents are more comfortable in Hindi, Punjabi, Gujarati, Urdu, Tamil, Bengali or another language, using it can make the conversation feel less formal and more like family.

How to tell parents about therapy

Many people wonder how to tell parents about therapy without alarming them. It can help to frame it in terms they already value:

  • Like a doctor for the mind. "When my back hurt I saw a physiotherapist. This is similar, for stress."
  • As a strength. "I want to handle my work and life better, and this helps me do that."
  • As private and professional. Therapy is confidential, and a therapist is not going to share family matters with anyone.
  • As not blaming them. "This is not about you doing something wrong. I just need some support right now."

A possible opening. "Mummy, Papa, I want to tell you something because I trust you. I have been feeling really low and anxious for a while, and I have started seeing someone who helps with stress. I am okay, and I do not need you to fix anything. I just did not want to keep it from you."

If it does not go well

First reactions are not always final ones. Parents may respond with worry, denial, advice or even anger, and then come back days later with a gentler question. Some useful ideas:

  • Give them time. You can say, "You do not have to respond now. We can talk again."
  • Stay with facts about your experience rather than debating whether mental health is "real".
  • Share one simple resource in their language if one exists, or offer to answer their questions.
  • Protect your care. You can continue therapy even if they do not fully understand yet.

If the conversation becomes unsafe, or you are pressured to stop getting help in ways that put you at risk, please reach out for support. Our guide on setting boundaries with South Asian parents may help.

How South Asian therapists can help

A therapist who understands South Asian families can help you rehearse the conversation, anticipate reactions, and cope with the guilt and fear that often come up. You will not have to explain why "just tell them" is not simple, or why log kya kahenge carries so much weight. Many clients look for an Indian therapist or another South Asian clinician for exactly this reason, while others find the right fit with any therapist who is deeply culturally informed. BIPOC therapy, at its best, treats your culture as context and strength rather than as a problem to fix.

Frequently asked questions

How do I tell my South Asian parents I am in therapy?

Choose a calm, private moment and start with feelings they will recognize, like stress or tiredness. Frame therapy as professional, confidential support, like seeing a doctor, and reassure them it is not about blaming them. Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

What if my parents do not believe in mental health?

Many parents grew up without that language. Focus on your experience rather than debating the concept, give them time, and remember you can continue your care as an adult even if they do not fully understand yet.

Do I need my parents' permission to see a therapist?

Adults do not need parental permission to start therapy, and sessions are confidential within legal and ethical limits. Your therapist will explain confidentiality at the start.

Should I look for South Asian therapists specifically?

It depends on what matters to you. Many clients find it easier with someone who shares or deeply understands their background. What matters most is a therapist who understands family and cultural context and does not treat your culture as the problem.

Can a therapist help me prepare for this conversation?

Yes. A therapist can help you clarify what you want to say, rehearse it, anticipate reactions and cope with the feelings that come up before and after.
