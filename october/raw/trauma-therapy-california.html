The short answer

Trauma therapy helps you process experiences that still feel present, so they lose some of their hold on your body, relationships and sense of self. Common types include EMDR, trauma-focused cognitive behavioral approaches, Internal Family Systems and body-based work such as Brainspotting. Good trauma care usually begins with safety and stabilization before any memory work. In California you can start in person or through secure telehealth.

Trauma is not defined only by what happened. It is also about how an experience was stored, and whether you had the support you needed at the time. Some people carry one clear event. Others carry years of smaller hurts: growing up walking on eggshells, being criticized or ignored, or being loved in a way that also frightened them. Both can shape how safe the world feels.

You might have come here after searching for a trauma therapist, trying an online CPTSD test at midnight, or wondering why you keep returning to someone who hurts you. This guide explains how trauma therapy works, the main types, what trauma bonding is, and how to start.

What trauma can look like

People respond to trauma in many ways, and only a qualified professional can assess what you are experiencing. Common experiences include:

  • Intrusive memories, nightmares or feeling as if it is happening again
  • Avoiding people, places or conversations that bring it back
  • Feeling constantly on edge, easily startled, or unable to relax
  • Numbness, disconnection or feeling far away from your life
  • Shame, self-blame or a sense of being fundamentally broken
  • Difficulty trusting others, or feeling drawn to relationships that repeat old pain

Childhood trauma in particular can show up in adulthood as people-pleasing, perfectionism, trouble with boundaries or a harsh inner critic, even when you do not think of your childhood as traumatic.

PTSD, complex PTSD and the CPTSD test

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a recognized condition that can follow a frightening or overwhelming event. The World Health Organization's ICD-11 also recognizes complex PTSD, which includes PTSD symptoms alongside ongoing difficulties with emotions, a deeply negative sense of self, and trouble in relationships. It is often linked to prolonged or repeated trauma, such as childhood abuse or long-term domestic violence.

Online, a CPTSD test is usually a short self-report questionnaire. Some, like the International Trauma Questionnaire, are used in research. They can be a useful prompt for reflection, but they cannot diagnose you. A licensed clinician looks at your history, context and daily life, and can help you make sense of the results with care.

What trauma bonding is

Trauma bonding describes a strong emotional attachment that can form in a relationship where harm and affection alternate. After cruelty or neglect comes warmth, an apology or intense closeness, and that relief can feel like love. Over time the cycle can make it very hard to leave, even when you know the relationship is hurting you. It is not a formal diagnosis, but it is a pattern many clinicians recognize.

Signs can include defending the person to friends, feeling you cannot live without them, blaming yourself for their behavior, or returning again and again despite promises to yourself. Our piece on breaking toxic relationship cycles explores this further.

How a therapist for trauma bonding helps

A therapist for trauma bonding does not tell you to "just leave". They help you understand the cycle and why it has such a strong pull, often linked to earlier attachment experiences. The work can include safety planning, grieving the relationship you hoped for, rebuilding trust in your own perceptions, and approaches like EMDR or Internal Family Systems for the deeper wounds underneath.

If you are not safe. If a partner or family member is hurting, threatening or controlling you, safety comes first. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is available at 1-800-799-7233, and you can call 911 in an emergency.

How does trauma therapy work?

Many trauma therapists follow a phased approach, often described in three broad stages:

  1. Safety and stabilization. Building coping skills, grounding and enough steadiness in daily life to do deeper work.
  2. Processing. Working with the memories, beliefs and body responses linked to trauma, at a pace you help set.
  3. Integration and reconnection. Rebuilding relationships, meaning and a sense of self that is not organized around the trauma.

You do not have to tell every detail to heal. A good trauma therapist will check in often, respect your pace, and help you end each session feeling more settled than when a hard memory came up.

Types of trauma therapy

  • EMDR. A structured approach using bilateral stimulation to help reprocess distressing memories. See what EMDR therapy is and how it works and our EMDR therapy page.
  • Trauma-focused cognitive behavioral approaches. Including cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure, which work with trauma-related beliefs and avoidance.
  • Internal Family Systems (IFS). Working gently with the protective parts of you and the parts still carrying pain.
  • Brainspotting and body-based work. Approaches that focus on how the nervous system holds trauma, including polyvagal-informed care.
  • Psychodynamic and narrative therapy. Exploring how the past shapes the present and the stories you tell about yourself.

Different types of trauma therapy suit different people. The right choice depends on your history, goals and what feels manageable.

Trauma, culture and family

For many immigrant and South Asian families, trauma can pass quietly between generations through survival habits, silence about hard histories, or pressure to keep going no matter what. Naming intergenerational patterns is not about blaming parents. It is about understanding what you inherited so you can choose what to carry forward. Culturally informed trauma counseling makes room for that context. Our guide to identity in South Asian communities touches on this.

Frequently asked questions

What does a therapist for trauma bonding do?

They help you understand the cycle of harm and affection that created the bond, why it pulls so strongly, and how earlier experiences may play a part. The work often includes safety planning, grief, rebuilding self-trust and deeper trauma work such as EMDR or IFS.

Can an online CPTSD test tell me if I have complex PTSD?

No. Online questionnaires can prompt reflection, but they cannot diagnose. A licensed clinician looks at your full history and daily life before offering any assessment, and can help you make sense of what you are experiencing.

How does trauma therapy work?

Many trauma therapists work in phases: first safety and stabilization, then processing painful memories and beliefs, then integration and reconnection. The pace is collaborative, and you do not have to share every detail to benefit.

What types of trauma therapy are there?

Common approaches include EMDR, trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapies such as cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure, Internal Family Systems, Brainspotting and other body-based methods, and psychodynamic or narrative therapy.

Can trauma therapy be done online?

Yes. Many people do trauma work through secure telehealth. We offer trauma therapy, including EMDR when clinically appropriate, both online for clients in California and in person in West Los Angeles and Pasadena.
