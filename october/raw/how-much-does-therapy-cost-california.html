The short answer

There is no single price in California: private-pay fees vary with the clinician's licence, experience, specialty and location. At our practice, 50-minute sessions generally range from $150 to $250. We do not bill insurance directly, but we can provide a superbill you may be able to submit for out-of-network reimbursement, and limited sliding-scale spots can be discussed.

Money is one of the most common reasons people put off therapy, and one of the least talked about. It can feel awkward to ask a therapist about fees, as if caring about the price means you do not take your mental health seriously. It does not. Knowing what you will pay, and how often you can realistically attend, is part of making therapy something you can keep doing rather than something you start and then quietly drop.

This guide walks through how much does therapy cost in California in practice, what drives the price, how insurance and superbills work, and the options that can make regular sessions more affordable. We use our own published rates as a real-world reference point, and we are happy to talk through your situation on a free consultation.

What drives the price of a therapy session

There is no single state rate. The therapy session cost you are quoted usually reflects a handful of factors:

  • Licence and training. Fully licensed clinicians (LMFT, LCSW, LPCC or psychologist) typically charge more than associates, who are pre-licensed clinicians practicing under supervision. Our guide to therapist vs psychologist explains the licence types.
  • Specialty. Specialized training such as EMDR, the Gottman Method or Emotionally Focused Therapy takes years to build, and fees often reflect it.
  • Session type. Couples and family sessions usually cost more than individual sessions because the work is more complex.
  • Location and format. Office rent in West Los Angeles is not the same as in a smaller town. Telehealth can sometimes widen your options across the state.

Does insurance cover therapy?

Often, at least in part, but how it works depends on whether your therapist is in network. California's mental health parity law (SB 855) requires state-regulated health plans to cover medically necessary treatment for mental health conditions, and federal parity rules apply to many employer plans as well. In practice, coverage still varies by plan, deductible and network.

Many private-practice therapists in California, including ours, are out of network. That means you pay the session fee directly, and we can give you a superbill: an itemized receipt with the codes your insurer needs. If your plan has out-of-network mental health benefits, you submit the superbill and may be reimbursed for part of the fee once your deductible is met.

Before you start, it is worth calling the number on your insurance card and asking:

  • Do I have out-of-network outpatient mental health benefits?
  • What is my out-of-network deductible, and how much of it have I met this year?
  • What percentage or amount is reimbursed per session after that?
  • Is telehealth reimbursed the same way as in-person sessions?
  • How do I submit a superbill, and how long does reimbursement usually take?

Is couples therapy covered by insurance?

Usually less often than individual therapy. Insurance generally reimburses treatment for a diagnosed condition in one person, and relationship difficulties on their own are not always treated as a medical necessity. Some plans do reimburse couples work when it is part of treating one partner's diagnosed condition, so it is worth asking your insurer directly rather than assuming. Our couples therapy sessions are private pay, and a superbill is available on request.

How much is therapy without insurance?

If you are paying fully out of pocket, you will usually pay the clinician's standard fee, which in our practice means $150 to $250 per session. Private pay has trade-offs. It costs more per session, but it also means no diagnosis has to be sent to an insurer, you are not limited to a network list, and you and your therapist decide together how long the work continues.

For many people, the real question is not the fee of one session but the monthly total. Weekly sessions at $175 add up to roughly $700 a month, while every other week is about half that. A therapist can talk with you about a rhythm that fits your goals and your budget, including starting weekly and spacing sessions out once things feel steadier.

Ways to make therapy more affordable

  • Ask about sliding scale. Some therapists, including ours, hold a limited number of reduced-fee spots. It never hurts to ask.
  • Consider an associate clinician. Associates are trained, supervised and often have more availability at a lower rate.
  • Check your employer's EAP. Many employers offer an Employee Assistance Program with a small number of free, short-term counseling sessions.
  • Use HSA or FSA funds. Many health savings and flexible spending plans treat therapy as an eligible expense. Check with your plan administrator.
  • Look at community options. County mental health departments, community clinics and university training clinics often offer lower-cost care.
  • Adjust frequency. Every other week can still be meaningful, especially once you have a working relationship.

A note on value. The cheapest option is not always the one you will stick with, and the most expensive is not automatically the best. What tends to matter most is fit: a therapist you trust, who understands your context, at a price you can keep up. What you pay for in a 50-minute session

A session is more than 50 minutes of conversation. The fee also covers the time your therapist spends on notes, treatment planning, consultation and ongoing training, plus the secure systems that protect your privacy. Is couples therapy covered by insurance?

Less often than individual therapy, because insurance usually reimburses treatment for a diagnosed condition in one person. Some plans reimburse couples work in specific circumstances, so it is best to ask your insurer directly. Our couples sessions are private pay, with a superbill available.

Do you offer a sliding scale?

We have limited sliding-scale availability, and we are glad to discuss it on your free consultation. Our associate clinicians also offer lower rates while working under clinical supervision.

It depends on your goals and history. Many people start weekly and later move to every other week. Your therapist will review progress with you regularly so the frequency keeps matching what you need and what you can sustain.
