The short answer

Therapy can help anxiety by teaching you to understand what triggers it, calm your nervous system in the moment, and gradually stop the avoidance that keeps anxiety going. Approaches such as cognitive behavioral therapy, exposure-based work, Internal Family Systems and EMDR are commonly used. In California, you can see a licensed therapist in person or through secure telehealth, and a free consultation is a low-pressure way to start.

Anxiety rarely announces itself neatly. For some people it is a racing mind at 2 a.m., rehearsing every conversation from the day. For others it shows up in the body first: a tight chest, a stomach that will not settle, a jaw clenched through meetings. Many of the professionals we work with are highly functional on the outside, which can make it hard to admit how much effort it takes to keep everything together.

If you have typed "anxiety therapist near me" into a search bar late at night, you are not alone, and you do not need to be in crisis to deserve support. This guide explains how therapy helps, what panic attacks are, when it makes sense to reach out, and what the first few sessions usually look like.

When to seek help for anxiety

Some anxiety is part of being human. It sharpens focus before a deadline and keeps us careful. It may be time to seek help for anxiety when:

  • Worry takes up more of your day than you would like, and it is hard to switch off
  • You are avoiding people, places or tasks because of how they make you feel
  • Sleep, concentration, appetite or relationships are being affected
  • Physical symptoms, such as a racing heart or tight chest, come on suddenly
  • You rely on alcohol, overworking or constant reassurance to cope

Only a qualified professional can assess what you are experiencing, and physical symptoms are worth checking with your doctor too, since some medical conditions can mimic anxiety. If you are unsure whether it is time, our guide on how to know if you need therapy can help you think it through.

Panic attacks and panic disorder

A panic attack is a sudden wave of intense fear or discomfort that usually peaks within minutes. It can bring a pounding heart, shortness of breath, dizziness, shaking, or a feeling that something terrible is about to happen. Many people go to an emergency room during their first attack, convinced it is a heart problem, and it is always reasonable to get checked.

A single panic attack is not the same thing as panic disorder. Clinicians consider panic disorder when attacks are recurrent and unexpected, and are followed by ongoing worry about having more or by changes in behavior to avoid them. A therapist for panic attacks will usually help you understand the cycle: a body sensation, a frightening interpretation, rising fear, and more sensations. Breaking that loop is very learnable.

A panic disorder therapist often uses cognitive behavioral therapy, including a gentle, gradual form of exposure to the feared sensations, so your body learns they are uncomfortable but not dangerous. Skills such as paced breathing and grounding help in the moment, while the deeper work reduces how often attacks arrive.

How does therapy help anxiety?

There is no single method, and a good therapist will choose one that fits you rather than the other way around. The approaches our clinicians commonly draw on include:

  • Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). Noticing anxious thought patterns, testing them, and practicing new responses.
  • Exposure-based work. Approaching feared situations step by step, so avoidance loosens its grip.
  • Internal Family Systems (IFS). Getting curious about the anxious "part" of you, which is often trying to protect you.
  • EMDR. Helpful when anxiety is rooted in earlier painful or frightening experiences. See our EMDR therapy page.
  • Polyvagal-informed and body-based care. Learning to recognize your nervous system's states and move back toward calm.

Therapists such as LMFTs do not prescribe medication. If you are curious whether medication could help alongside therapy, a psychiatrist or your primary care doctor is the right person to ask, and your therapist can coordinate with them if you wish.

Anxiety, culture and high achievement

For many South Asian, BIPOC and first-generation clients, anxiety is tangled up with family expectations, the pressure to succeed on everyone's behalf, and a fear of being seen as ungrateful or weak. In some families, anxiety is described only in physical terms, or not described at all. That can make it harder to recognize and harder to ask for help.

Culturally informed therapy makes room for that context without treating your culture as the problem. You should not have to explain why you cannot simply "say no" to a parent, or why a single performance review feels like it carries the weight of your family's sacrifices. Our South Asian therapy page and our guide to therapy for high achievers go deeper on this.

What your first sessions look like

The first session is mostly about understanding: what the anxiety feels like, when it started, what makes it better or worse, and what you want to be different. Over the next few sessions, you and your therapist agree on goals and begin building skills you can use right away. Read what to expect in your first therapy session for a fuller picture.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find an anxiety therapist near me in California?

Look for a licensed clinician whose specialties include anxiety, check their approach and fees, and book a consultation to see whether you feel comfortable. Telehealth widens your options to therapists anywhere in California. We see clients in West Los Angeles, Pasadena and online statewide.

Can therapy help with panic attacks?

Many people find therapy very helpful for panic attacks. Approaches such as cognitive behavioral therapy and gradual exposure to feared sensations can reduce how often attacks happen and how frightening they feel. It is also wise to see a doctor to rule out a medical cause for physical symptoms.

How long does therapy for anxiety take?

It varies with your goals and history. Some people notice changes within a few months, while others choose longer-term work to address deeper patterns. Your therapist will review progress with you regularly.

Is online therapy for anxiety as helpful as in person?

Research on telehealth generally supports online therapy as effective for many people with anxiety, and it can be easier to attend consistently. Some people prefer an office setting. You can discuss which format suits you during your free consultation.

Do you prescribe medication for anxiety?

No. Our clinicians provide therapy and do not prescribe medication. If you want to explore medication, speak with a psychiatrist or your primary care doctor. Your therapist can coordinate with them if you give permission.
