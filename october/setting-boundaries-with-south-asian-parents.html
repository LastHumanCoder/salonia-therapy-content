Setting Boundaries With South Asian Parents: A Therapist's Perspective

The short answer

Setting boundaries with South Asian parents usually works best when it is warm, specific and consistent. Name what you will and will not do, rather than what they must change, expect some pushback and guilt, and keep showing love in the ways that feel true to you. A boundary is not rejection. It is a way of staying connected without losing yourself. A culturally informed therapist can help you plan the conversation and cope with the guilt afterwards.

You can love your parents deeply and still feel suffocated by their expectations. You can be grateful for everything they gave up and still need them to stop calling six times a day, commenting on your weight, or asking when you will get married. For many children of South Asian immigrants, holding both of those truths at once is one of the hardest parts of adult life.

Western advice on boundaries can feel like it was written for a different family. "Just tell them no" does not account for duty, sacrifice, joint families or the question of what the community will say. This guide offers a therapist's view of setting boundaries in a way that respects your culture and your own well-being, and explains when it may help to talk with an Indian therapist near me or a South Asian therapist who already understands the context.

Why boundaries feel so hard in South Asian families

Many South Asian families are built around closeness and interdependence. Those are real strengths. They can also make individual limits feel like betrayal. Some patterns we hear often:

  • Sacrifice as a debt. "We came here for you" can make any disagreement feel like ingratitude.
  • Log kya kahenge. Worry about what relatives and the community will say shapes decisions about careers, marriage and even mental health.
  • Blurry roles. Adult children may be expected to translate, manage finances, care for siblings or mediate between parents.
  • Love shown through control. For many parents, advice, worry and involvement are how love is expressed.
  • Guilt as a signal. Many clients describe feeling guilty for simply having preferences.

It also helps to remember that many immigrant parents carry their own unspoken stress and loss. Understanding that does not mean you have to accept every demand, but it can soften how you approach the conversation.

A boundary is about you, not about controlling them

A boundary is a statement about what you will do, not a rule about what your parents must do. "You cannot comment on my career" is a demand they can ignore. "If the conversation turns to my career, I am going to change the subject or end the call" is a boundary you can keep. That shift matters, because you cannot control whether your South Asian parents agree. You can only control how you respond.

Common areas where adult children set boundaries include:

  • Time and contact: how often you call, visit or reply
  • Privacy: what you share about relationships, money or health
  • Decisions: career, marriage, where you live and how you raise children
  • Money: what financial support you can and cannot offer
  • Comments: remarks about weight, appearance, age or marital status

How to set a boundary with your parents

  1. Get clear on one boundary first. Pick the issue that costs you the most, rather than trying to change everything at once.
  2. Lead with the relationship. Start with what you value: "I love you and I want us to be close."
  3. Be specific and brief. Long explanations invite debate. Say what will happen, kindly.
  4. Choose timing and language. A calm moment works better than the middle of an argument. If your parents are more at ease in Hindi, Punjabi, Urdu, Tamil or another language, saying it in their language can soften it.
  5. Follow through. Consistency is what teaches a boundary. One gentle, repeated action does more than one big speech.

Words you might try. "Mummy, I know you ask because you worry. I am not going to talk about marriage on our calls anymore. I would love to hear about your week instead." Or: "Papa, I can help with the bills this month, but I cannot keep doing it every month. Let us sit down together and look at other options."

When they push back

Expect some resistance. When a long-standing pattern changes, many families push harder at first: more calls, tears, silent treatment, or relatives getting involved. This does not mean the boundary failed. It often means it is being noticed. Stay kind, stay consistent, and keep offering connection in other ways, such as a weekly call you enjoy or a meal together.

Guilt is likely to show up too. Guilt is a feeling, not proof that you have done something wrong. Many of our clients find it helps to ask: "Am I harming them, or am I disappointing them?" Disappointing someone you love is painful, but it is not the same as hurting them.

If setting a boundary is met with threats, violence or control, your safety comes first. Please reach out for support, and if you are in danger, call 911.

How therapy helps

A therapist cannot set boundaries for you, but they can help you figure out what you actually want, rehearse hard conversations, and sit with the guilt without being ruled by it. Approaches such as Internal Family Systems can help with the part of you that feels responsible for everyone's happiness. You can read more about this on our South Asian therapy page and in our guide to identity issues in South Asian communities.

For many people, working with an Indian therapist, a South Asian therapist, or any BIPOC therapist who understands collectivist families means not having to explain why "just move out" is not simple. The goal of BIPOC therapy is not to pull you away from your culture. It is to help you find a way of belonging that includes you. Frequently asked questions

Is setting boundaries with parents disrespectful in South Asian culture?

It can feel that way, but boundaries can be set with warmth and respect. A boundary describes what you will do, not a rejection of your parents. Many people find their relationships become more honest and less resentful once boundaries are in place.

How do I deal with guilt after setting a boundary?

Expect guilt and treat it as a feeling rather than proof you did something wrong. Ask yourself whether you are harming your parents or disappointing them. Therapy can help you work through guilt so it does not decide for you.

What if my parents refuse to accept my boundary?

Pushback is common at first. Stay calm, keep the boundary consistently, and continue to offer connection in other ways. You cannot control their reaction, only your response. If there are threats or violence, prioritize your safety.

Do I need a South Asian therapist to work on family boundaries?

Not necessarily, but you do need a therapist who understands collectivist family dynamics and does not treat your culture as the problem. Many clients find it easier with someone who shares or deeply understands their background.
